How to Find a Property Broker in Dubai: What Expats Need to Know

Published October 2026 • Dubai Real Estate Directory — Independent

Dubai's property market is open to foreign buyers and expat residents — but finding the right broker requires understanding a licensing system, fee structure, and transaction process that work differently from most Western markets. This guide explains how it all works.

What RERA is and why it matters

RERA — the Real Estate Regulatory Agency — is the division of the Dubai Land Department (DLD) that licenses and oversees real estate brokers in Dubai. Every agent and agency operating legally in Dubai must hold a current RERA licence.

A RERA licence requires passing the RERA exam, paying a registration fee, and renewing annually. An agent whose licence has lapsed is not legally permitted to practice — and if a transaction goes wrong with an unlicensed agent, you have limited legal recourse.

This is not bureaucratic box-ticking. In a market where off-plan payments can involve hundreds of thousands of dirhams, working with an unlicensed broker is a meaningful risk.

How to verify: The DLD maintains a public register of licensed brokers at dubailand.gov.ae. Search by agency name or ORN (Office Registration Number). Always verify before signing anything.

How to find a broker in Dubai

There is no shortage of brokers in Dubai — the city has thousands of licensed agents. The challenge is not finding one; it is finding the right one for your specific situation.

The most effective approaches:

  • Start with the agency, not the agent. Research which agencies specialise in the type of property and area you are looking at. Then ask for an agent who focuses on that specific community or property type within that agency.
  • Use the directory to compare. Dubai Real Estate Directory profiles 24 of the most established agencies in the market, with consistent information on what each one specialises in, the areas they cover, and their Google ratings. Compare before contacting.
  • Ask your employer or HR. Large organisations with significant expat workforces often have relationships with agencies they recommend to incoming staff. This is not a guarantee of quality, but it provides accountability.
  • Ask in expat community groups. Dubai has large expat online communities (Facebook groups, Reddit, local WhatsApp groups) where people share agency recommendations. Recent, specific recommendations from people with similar requirements are more useful than general rankings.

Questions to ask before choosing a broker

Before committing to a broker, ask these questions. The answers tell you more than any review.

  • "What is your RERA ORN and can I see your licence?" A legitimate agent will provide this without hesitation. An agent who deflects or becomes defensive is a red flag.
  • "How many transactions have you completed in [specific community] in the last 12 months?" This tests whether the agent has genuine local knowledge or is just willing to take your enquiry. An agent who cannot answer concretely may not be the right fit.
  • "Are you working with any developers on this property?" If you are looking at off-plan, the agent may receive a developer commission on top of buyer commission. This is legal and common, but you should know about it — it creates an incentive to steer you toward specific launches.
  • "What are your total fees, and when are they due?" Get the full fee structure in writing. Standard is 2% of the purchase price. Any additions should be explained before you sign a Form A.
  • "What happens if the transaction falls through before completion?" Understand upfront what is refundable and what is not.

How broker fees work in Dubai

Dubai's fee structure is different from many Western markets:

  • Standard commission: 2% of purchase price — paid by the buyer. On a AED 1.5m apartment, this is AED 30,000 (approximately $8,200). Some agencies have a minimum fee floor (typically AED 10,000–15,000).
  • Transfer fees: 4% to Dubai Land Department — this is the main transaction tax, paid at the DLD at the point of transfer. It is separate from agent commission and non-negotiable.
  • Admin fees: varies — some agencies charge administration fees (AED 2,000–5,000) on top of commission. Others include it in the commission. Ask upfront.
  • Developer commission for off-plan: usually paid by developer — for off-plan property, agents typically receive 3–5% commission directly from the developer. As a buyer, you may pay 2% to the agent in addition, or the developer may absorb the full agent cost. Confirm before proceeding.

Negotiating commission below 2% is generally not worth the friction. An agent working at 1% is likely spending proportionally less time on your transaction. The 2% is what funds the process you need.

What the buying process looks like

For a secondary market (resale) purchase, the process typically runs as follows:

01

Property search and viewing

Identify properties through your agent, Bayut, Property Finder, or directly through agency listings. View in person before making any offer.

02

Offer and negotiation

Make an offer through your agent. Standard in Dubai is to negotiate on price but not typically on agent commission. Offers are verbal until the MOU is signed.

03

Memorandum of Understanding (Form F)

Once price is agreed, a Form F (the DLD's official MOU) is signed. You typically pay a 10% deposit at this stage, held in trust. The MOU sets the completion timeline (usually 30–60 days for cash, 90 days for mortgage).

04

NOC from developer

For properties in developer communities, a No Objection Certificate (NOC) from the developer is required before transfer. Your agent organises this. It can take 1–2 weeks.

05

Transfer at DLD

Both buyer and seller (or their authorised representatives) attend the DLD office. The 4% transfer fee is paid. Ownership is registered to the buyer. The process takes a few hours.

Red flags to watch for

Dubai's market attracts aggressive tactics. These are warning signs:

If an agent cannot or will not provide their RERA licence number and ORN, stop the conversation. Working with an unlicensed agent is a legal and financial risk.

  • Pressure on urgency — "This property will be gone by tomorrow" is sometimes true and often not. Manufactured urgency is a tactic to prevent you doing proper due diligence.
  • Requests for cash payments — All payments in Dubai real estate should go through manager's cheques or bank transfers, not cash. Legitimate transactions leave paper trails.
  • Custom contract templates instead of Form F — Form F is the DLD's official MOU and provides legal protections. An agency insisting on their own contract instead is a serious red flag.
  • Refusing to let you verify RERA status — "Trust me, I'm licensed" is not verification. The DLD register is public and takes 30 seconds to check.
  • One-size-fits-all recommendations — An agent recommending the same community or building regardless of your brief is probably steering you toward inventory they have on their books, not looking for your best fit.
  • No documentation at any stage — Get everything in writing. Verbal agreements mean nothing at the DLD.

Freehold vs leasehold — what expats need to know

Not all properties in Dubai can be owned outright by foreign nationals. Dubai has designated freehold zones where foreigners can buy and own property outright (no time limit, no nationality restriction). Outside these zones, foreigners can sometimes purchase on long leases (50 or 99 years) — this is leasehold, and you are buying time, not title.

Most of the properties that expats are looking at — Dubai Marina, Downtown Dubai, Palm Jumeirah, JBR, Business Bay, Arabian Ranches, Dubai Hills Estate, Jumeirah Village Circle — are in freehold zones. But if you are looking at a property outside these established areas, confirm its ownership structure before proceeding.

Your broker should know which zones are freehold without hesitation. An agent who is uncertain about this for a property they are showing you is an agent who is not a specialist in that area.
