Dubai Property Service Charges Explained: What to Check Before Buying (2026)

Updated November 2026 · Dubai Real Estate Directory

Service charges are the annual maintenance fee every owner in a Dubai strata building or community pays. They are unavoidable, they vary enormously — from under AED 8 per square foot in budget areas to over AED 40 in premium towers — and they directly affect your net rental yield and long-term holding costs. Here is everything you need to know before buying.

What are Dubai property service charges?

When you buy a property in Dubai — an apartment in a tower, a villa in a gated community, or a unit in a mixed-use development — you become a co-owner of the shared infrastructure: the lobby, lifts, pool, gym, security systems, landscaping, and the building fabric itself. Service charges are your annual contribution toward maintaining, operating, and eventually replacing all of that.

The charges are collected by the building's Owners Association (OA) or community management company and held in two funds: the general maintenance fund for day-to-day running costs, and the reserve fund for major capital expenditures like lift replacements or roof repairs. Both are mandatory under UAE law.

Service charges are set annually, usually at a general meeting of the Owners Association. They are regulated by the Real Estate Regulatory Agency (RERA), which publishes a service charge index that sets indicative benchmarks for each area and building category. RERA's index is intended to prevent overcharging, though in practice charges in premium buildings can run significantly above it.

Key point: Service charges are a legal obligation, not optional. They attach to the property, not the owner — if you buy a property with outstanding service charge arrears, those arrears may become your liability. Always request a clearance certificate before completing any purchase.

How are service charges calculated?

Service charges in Dubai are calculated on a per-square-foot basis, applied to your unit's registered area (the total internal area plus an allocated share of common areas, sometimes called the "sellable area" or "unit area" on the Title Deed).

The annual charge equals the building's approved rate per square foot multiplied by your unit's registered area. A 1,000 sq ft apartment in a building with a rate of AED 15/sq ft would generate an annual service charge bill of AED 15,000.

The rate itself is set by a Service Charge Budget prepared by the building's management company and approved by RERA. The budget must cover:

  • Facilities management (cleaning, security, pest control, landscaping)
  • Utility costs for shared areas (electricity, water, chiller if centrally supplied)
  • Insurance for the building structure and common areas
  • Minor repairs and maintenance throughout the year
  • Reserve fund contribution (typically 10–20% of the total budget)
  • Management company fees

Bills are typically issued quarterly or annually and must be paid within 30 days of the invoice date.

District cooling (chilled water for air conditioning, supplied centrally) is a major driver of higher service charges in certain buildings. Buildings connected to district cooling networks in Business Bay, Downtown Dubai, and JLT often have a separate district cooling charge on top of the standard service charge — this can add AED 5–12 per square foot per year to your effective cost.

District cooling: In buildings with centrally supplied district cooling (chilled water A/C), the district cooling charge is separate from the service charge but equally unavoidable. Confirm whether a building uses district cooling before buying — it significantly affects your total annual holding cost and therefore your net yield.

What do service charges cover?

The standard service charge covers all shared areas and building-wide systems. What you get for your money depends on what the building has:

Always included

  • Building cleaning and common area maintenance
  • Security staff and access control systems
  • Lift maintenance and breakdown service
  • Fire safety system testing and certification
  • Building structure insurance
  • Shared utility costs (lobby electricity, water for common areas)
  • Reserve fund (for future major capital works)

Included in mid-to-premium buildings

  • Gym and pool maintenance and staffing
  • Landscaping and outdoor areas
  • Concierge or reception desk
  • Parking management
  • CCTV monitoring

Not included — your separate cost as an owner

  • Your unit's own DEWA (electricity and water) usage
  • District cooling charges for your unit (billed separately by the cooling provider)
  • Your unit's own maintenance and repairs (broken appliances, plumbing inside the unit)
  • Contents insurance

A common misunderstanding is that "low service charges mean low overheads." This is only true if the building has the facilities to match. A tower with a large pool, fully equipped gym and hotel-style concierge that charges AED 20/sq ft is better value than a building with limited amenities charging AED 15/sq ft, depending on how you use those facilities.

How to check service charges before buying

Checking the service charge on a specific unit before buying is straightforward — but buyers often skip this step and discover a nasty surprise after they've signed the MOU.

Three ways to verify service charge rates:

  1. Ask the seller for recent service charge invoices. A legitimate seller will have these. The invoice shows the rate per square foot and any arrears.
  2. Check the RERA service charge index. RERA publishes a service charge index on the Dubai Land Department website (available through the DLD's online portals). This shows the approved benchmark rate for the specific building by its registration number. It may not reflect mid-year increases but gives a baseline.
  3. Ask the building's Owners Association or management company directly. They are obliged to provide this information to prospective buyers. Contact details are usually on the building's noticeboard or the RERA portal.
Arrears clearance: Before completing any purchase, insist on a service charge clearance certificate from the building's management company or the DLD's escrow portal. This confirms the unit has zero outstanding arrears. The DLD trustee office will typically request this document at transfer — but do not wait until transfer day to discover a problem.

If service charge arrears exist on the property, they typically need to be settled before the No Objection Certificate (NOC) can be issued by the developer. In practice, this is often deducted from the seller's proceeds at transfer — but it can delay the transaction if the amount is disputed or the seller is uncooperative.

Service charges and rental yield

Service charges are the largest unavoidable cost of owning an investment property in Dubai, and they have a direct and significant impact on your net rental yield. Many investors focus on gross yield — annual rent divided by purchase price — without accounting for the service charge drag.

The gap between gross and net yield widens significantly in premium buildings. A Downtown Dubai apartment with a gross yield of 5.5% and a service charge of AED 28/sq ft on 1,100 sq ft generates AED 30,800 in annual service charges alone — which could cut the net yield to under 4%. In a low-yield, high-service-charge scenario, you can easily find yourself holding a property that barely covers its own maintenance costs.

The rule of thumb: in investment-focused areas like JVC and Dubai Sports City, where service charges are lower and yields are higher, net yield is closer to gross. In premium areas like Downtown and Palm Jumeirah, the gap is larger. This is part of why high-gross-yield properties in mid-market areas often represent better pure income investments than premium properties.

What happens if you don't pay service charges?

Non-payment of service charges in Dubai carries real consequences. RERA gives Owners Associations and management companies tools to enforce collection:

  • Late payment penalty: Most buildings charge a penalty interest rate (typically 12% per annum) on overdue balances from the due date.
  • Access restrictions: The management company can restrict access to amenities (gym, pool, parking) for owners with outstanding balances.
  • Legal action: The Owners Association can file a claim with the Rental Disputes Settlement Centre (RDSC) or pursue civil court action to recover arrears plus costs.
  • Title Deed restriction: In serious cases, RERA can place a restriction on the Title Deed that prevents the owner from selling the property until arrears are settled. This is a significant enforcement tool — a blocked Title Deed makes the property unsellable.

Practical note: The building's ability to pursue you depends partly on how well-organised its Owners Association is. In some buildings, collection is aggressive and efficient; in others, arrears accumulate for years without consequence. This is a reason to check the financial health of a building's OA before buying — an OA with large outstanding arrears across multiple units often indicates deferred maintenance and poorly managed reserves.

Disputing service charges

If you believe the service charge levied is incorrect, excessive, or not in line with the RERA-approved budget, you have recourse through the official process:

  1. Raise it with the management company first. Most disputes are administrative errors (wrong unit size on file, duplicate charges) and are resolved quickly.
  2. Escalate to the Owners Association. If the management company is unresponsive, the OA is the governing body and can instruct the management company.
  3. File a complaint with RERA. RERA's Strata & Building Management section handles disputes. You can file online via the DLD website. RERA has the authority to audit the service charge budget, reduce charges it finds unjustified, and sanction management companies for non-compliance.

Disputes over the amount are different from disputes over arrears (which you owe but are contesting). Pay the undisputed portion under protest to avoid enforcement action while the dispute is resolved.

Key questions to ask your agent before buying

A good agent will have these answers ready. If they don't, that itself is a signal about how well they know the building.

  • What is the current approved service charge rate per square foot for this building?
  • Has the rate increased in the past three years? By how much?
  • Does the building use district cooling? If so, what is the typical annual district cooling charge for a unit of this size?
  • Is the reserve fund adequately funded, or has the OA been deferring contributions?
  • Are there any outstanding major capital works planned (lift replacement, façade repair, pool renovation) that may result in a special levy?
  • Can the seller provide service charge clearance documentation confirming zero arrears?
  • What is the current net yield after service charges for comparable rented units in this building?

These are not unusual questions — any experienced agent advising investors will expect them. If your agent can't answer them or becomes evasive, consider working with a specialist who knows the investment market more deeply.
